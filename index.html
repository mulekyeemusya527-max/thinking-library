The Purpose of Human Beings on Earth Essay

Faith Mulekye, The Thinking Library · Published 27 November 2026 · 4 min read

MeaningPhilosophy of religionExistentialism

Introduction

One of the oldest questions humanity has ever asked is: Why are we here? What is the purpose of human existence? Throughout history, religions, philosophers, and scientists have attempted to explain why human beings exist and what they are meant to accomplish. Some believe that humanity was created by God for a specific purpose, while others argue that life has no predetermined meaning and that individuals must create their own purpose. The question of human existence therefore raises important debates about creation, morality, freedom, happiness, and the meaning of life.

1. Religious Perspectives

Christianity: Christianity teaches that human beings were created in the image of God (Genesis 1:27). This means humanity has inherent dignity and a responsibility to reflect God's character through love, kindness, justice, and compassion. According to Christianity, the purpose of life is to know God, worship Him, obey His commandments, love others, and care for creation. Jesus summarises this purpose through the two greatest commandments: loving God and loving one's neighbour (Matthew 22:37–40). From this perspective, wealth, fame, and pleasure are temporary, whereas spiritual growth and eternal life are of greater significance.

Islam: Islam teaches that human beings were created to worship Allah (Quran 51:56). Worship, however, extends beyond prayer and religious rituals; it includes honesty, justice, generosity, acquiring knowledge, helping others, and fulfilling one's responsibilities. Human beings are also regarded as stewards of the earth, entrusted with protecting creation and establishing justice.

African Traditional Religions: Many African traditional religious perspectives understand human existence through relationships with the community, ancestors, nature, and the spiritual world. The philosophy of Ubuntu, expressed through the phrase "I am because we are," emphasises that life becomes meaningful through relationships, cooperation, belonging, and contributing to the well-being of others.

2. Philosophical Perspectives

Aristotle: The ancient Greek philosopher Aristotle argued that the ultimate purpose of human existence is eudaimonia, meaning happiness, flourishing, or living well. He believed that genuine happiness does not come from temporary pleasure, wealth, or power but from developing virtues such as courage, wisdom, honesty, moderation, and justice. Human beings fulfil their potential by using reason and becoming morally excellent individuals. Therefore, the purpose of life is not simply to enjoy oneself but to become the best version of oneself.

Existentialism: Philosophers such as Jean-Paul Sartre and Albert Camus questioned whether human beings have a predetermined purpose. Sartre argued that "existence precedes essence," meaning that individuals first exist and then define themselves through their choices and actions. Camus explored the possibility that the universe has no inherent meaning, despite humanity's desire to find one. He argued that people should confront this reality and continue living courageously. From this perspective, purpose is not necessarily something given to us; it is something we create through our decisions, relationships, and experiences.

3. Atheist Perspectives

Atheism does not necessarily mean believing that life is meaningless. Many atheists argue that human beings emerged through natural processes, particularly biological evolution, rather than supernatural creation. From this perspective, there is no established scientific evidence that humanity was created to fulfil a predetermined divine mission. Human existence can be explained through evolutionary processes involving survival, adaptation, and reproduction. However, explaining how humans came into existence does not necessarily answer what makes their lives meaningful.

Secular humanists and atheist thinkers argue that people can create meaningful lives through love, relationships, scientific discovery, creativity, raising children, and improving society. Philosopher Bertrand Russell believed humanity should pursue knowledge and understanding while confronting the universe honestly. From this perspective, human beings do not need a supernatural purpose to live meaningful lives. Their purpose can come from reducing suffering, developing knowledge, helping others, and contributing positively to future generations.

Conclusion

The purpose of human existence remains one of humanity's greatest philosophical questions. Religion generally teaches that life has a divine purpose, philosophy explores meaning through reason, virtue, and freedom, while atheism argues that meaningful existence is possible without a supernatural creator. Although these perspectives disagree about where human purpose originates, they all raise important questions about how people should live.

Perhaps the most important question is not simply why we exist but what we choose to do with our existence. If life is a gift from God, we have a responsibility to honour it. If life is the result of natural processes, we still have the opportunity to make it meaningful. Ultimately, human beings have the ability to love, think, create, make choices, and influence the world. The significance of our existence may therefore lie not only in discovering where we came from but also in deciding what kind of world we want to leave behind.

Is Morality Subjective or Objective? Essay

What Is the Origin of Good and Evil?

Faith Mulekye, The Thinking Library · Published 27 November 2026 · 5 min read

EthicsMoralityPhilosophy of religion

Introduction

One of the most difficult questions in philosophy, religion, and human existence is whether morality is objective or subjective. What makes an action good or evil? Is murder wrong because God prohibits it, because society condemns it, because it causes suffering, or because it violates a universal moral truth? If an entire society agreed that enslaving innocent people was acceptable, would slavery suddenly become morally right? This question exposes a fundamental debate: Are good and evil universal realities, or are they human interpretations shaped by culture, religion, evolution, and personal beliefs?

1. Religious Perspectives

Christianity: Christianity teaches that God is the ultimate source of goodness, justice, and truth. Moral principles originate from God's nature and are communicated through divine commandments and human conscience. The Ten Commandments, for example, prohibit murder, theft, and false testimony. However, Christianity also faces an important philosophical challenge known as the Euthyphro dilemma, originally associated with Plato: Is something good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good? Some Christian philosophers argue that goodness is grounded in God's unchanging nature rather than arbitrary commands.

Islam: Islam teaches that Allah is the ultimate authority on morality, establishing principles of justice, compassion, honesty, and respect for human life (Quran 16:90). Islamic thinkers have also debated whether moral truths can be discovered through human reason or whether they depend primarily on divine revelation.

African Traditional Religions: Many African traditional religious perspectives connect morality to community, spiritual order, ancestors, and social harmony. Actions are considered good when they promote cooperation, respect, responsibility, and communal well-being. Evil is associated with actions that destroy relationships, encourage disorder, or harm others. Although African traditions are diverse, many emphasise that morality is relational: individuals have responsibilities not only to themselves but also to their families, communities, and the wider spiritual world.

2. Philosophical Perspectives

Plato: Plato argued that goodness exists independently of human opinions. Through his theory of Forms, he proposed that the Form of the Good represents an ultimate and unchanging standard against which human actions can be judged. Therefore, an action does not become good simply because society approves of it.

Immanuel Kant: Kant argued that morality should be grounded in human reason rather than personal desires or social approval. His categorical imperative states that individuals should act only according to principles they could rationally wish to become universal laws. For example, if everyone lied whenever it benefited them, trust would disappear. Kant also argued that human beings should be treated as ends in themselves, never merely as instruments for another person's benefit.

Utilitarianism: Philosophers Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill argued that morality should be evaluated according to the consequences of actions. An action is morally right when it produces the greatest overall happiness and reduces suffering. For example, helping vulnerable people is good because it improves well-being. However, utilitarianism raises difficult questions about whether harming one individual can ever be justified if doing so benefits many others. This illustrates the tension between collective happiness and individual rights.

3. Atheist Perspectives

Many atheists argue that morality does not require belief in God. They suggest that moral behaviour developed through evolution because human beings are social creatures who depend on cooperation for survival. Over generations, tendencies such as empathy, fairness, generosity, protection of children, and reciprocal behaviour may have helped human communities survive. From this perspective, moral behaviour has natural origins rather than necessarily supernatural ones. However, explaining how moral tendencies evolved does not automatically establish what is morally right.

Atheist thinkers offer different explanations of moral principles. Sam Harris argues that morality can be grounded in the well-being of conscious creatures, meaning actions that increase suffering and destroy well-being are morally wrong. Richard Dawkins explores how evolutionary processes can explain cooperation, generosity, and reciprocal behaviour. Nevertheless, atheism does not automatically imply moral subjectivism. An atheist can believe that certain actions, such as torture, slavery, and the deliberate killing of innocent people, are objectively wrong without believing in God.

4. The Problem of Evil and the Existence of Suffering

A major philosophical challenge to religious morality is the problem of evil. If God is all-powerful, all-knowing, and perfectly good, why does innocent suffering exist? Why do children suffer, why do natural disasters destroy lives, and why do human beings commit terrible atrocities? One religious response is the free-will defence, which argues that God gave humanity freedom to choose between good and evil. Genuine freedom makes love and kindness possible but also allows cruelty and injustice. Other religious thinkers suggest that suffering can create opportunities for courage, compassion, and spiritual development. However, these explanations face difficulties when dealing with apparently unnecessary suffering.

Atheistic philosophers argue that the existence of immense suffering presents a serious challenge to the idea of an all-powerful and perfectly good God. They may interpret evil not as a supernatural force but as actions and conditions that cause suffering, destroy well-being, or violate human dignity. From this perspective, humanity has a responsibility to reduce suffering through reason, compassion, justice, and collective action. The debate remains unresolved, demonstrating the difficulty of reconciling the existence of suffering with different understandings of divine goodness.

Conclusion

The origin of good and evil remains one of the deepest questions in philosophy and human existence. Religious traditions generally locate morality in God, divine revelation, or spiritual principles. Philosophers such as Plato and Kant defend forms of objective morality grounded in independent truths or human reason. Subjectivists argue that morality depends on personal attitudes and cultural values, while atheistic thinkers often explain moral behaviour through evolution, empathy, rationality, and human well-being.

However, explaining where moral beliefs originate is not necessarily the same as establishing whether those beliefs are true. Evolution may explain why humans developed empathy, but does it prove that compassion is morally right? Religion may explain morality through God, but is something good only because God commands it? Society may establish laws, but does legality automatically make something morally acceptable? My conclusion is that there are strong reasons to believe that at least some moral principles should apply universally. Torturing innocent people, exploiting vulnerable individuals, and unjustly destroying human life should not become acceptable simply because a society approves of them. Whether these principles originate from God, human reason, nature, or a combination of these remains open to debate. Ultimately, morality should help humanity protect dignity, reduce suffering, promote justice, and coexist peacefully.

About and editorial policy

The Thinking Library exists to make knowledge accessible and to encourage independent thought. It informs readers and does not tell them what to believe.

How content is labelled

  • Established facts: supported by the evidence cited.
  • Interpretations: explanations that reasonable people can contest.
  • Disputed claims: claims on which sources or experts disagree.
  • Open questions: what is not yet known.

News reports events. Essays, research notes and commentary are kept in a separate section.

Principles

  • Competing arguments are presented fairly, without false balance where evidence is strong.
  • Headlines are factual, not sensational.
  • Uncertainty and corrections are stated openly.
  • Sources are named and linked; unverifiable claims are excluded or labelled.
